“The first man who fenced off a piece of land and dared to say, ‘This is mine,’ and who found people simple enough to believe him, was the true founder of civil society.”

— Jean-Jacques Rousseau, Discourse on the Origin of Inequality(1755)

15. July 2026

July 14, 2026. The state capital of Munich issues a general order. Mayor Dominik Krause has signed it, and Stadtwerke München (SWM) follows suit with its own water conservation order, which extends to the surrounding municipalities. The reason: 360 million liters of water consumed per day—60 million more than usual. In the city’s press photo, a sprinkler is running in broad daylight. Water glistens in the sun; some of it evaporates before it reaches the ground. No one in this photo is doing anything illegal—not yet. But from now on, they are.

The Setup

Munich is once again using 360 million liters of water a day instead of 300 million. The city is responding the way cities respond: with a general order. No watering lawns between 9 a.m. and 7 p.m., no swimming pools, no car washing in private driveways—with fines of up to 50,000 euros. Every single rule makes sense. Every single watering can that’s used anyway is reasonable, too. No one is acting out of malice. No one wants to dry out Munich. And that’s exactly the problem: a resource that belongs to no one is being used by everyone at the same time—reasonably, understandably, unstoppably. Rousseau would have assumed the opposite. The general order proves why he was only half right.

The Lawn Sprinkler as a Model

Technically speaking, running a lawn sprinkler at noon is a waste. Most of the water evaporates into the air before it reaches the roots. Every gardener knows this. Nevertheless, in the summer, millions of sprinklers run precisely when the sun is at its highest—not out of ignorance, but due to a lack of time. In the morning, there’s rush hour; in the evening, people are getting off work, but not until after nine. The sprinkler runs when there’s time for it, not when it would make sense.

That’s the microcosm of the whole problem. The individual decision—to water now, not to wait—is completely rational for the person doing the watering. They see their lawn, their time, their watering can. They don’t see the water table beneath the city, which they share with one and a half million other decisions they neither see nor know about. The resource is invisible until it disappears. By the time a general order makes it visible, it’s already in short supply.

The order, specifically

The facts are clear. Daily consumption of over 360 million liters compared to a typical average of 300 million—an increase of one-fifth, which may seem moderate on paper but is critical from a hydrological perspective. Effective July 14, 2026, the following activities, among others, are prohibited: filling private pools, fountains, and water barrels; watering lawns and green spaces; watering gardens between 9:00 a.m. and 7:00 p.m., except via drip irrigation; washing cars outside of car washes; and drawing water from lakes and rivers within the city limits. Violations may be penalized with fines of up to 50,000 euros under the Water Resources Act. SWM, under its managing director Florian Bieberbach, is extending the regulation to the entire supply network, beyond the city limits. The order is temporary, valid until August 1, 2026—with an explicit option for extension “in the event of persistent drought.” This last phrase deserves attention. It is not an exception. It is the basic assumption, packaged in a subordinate clause.

Why No One Is to Blame

Why does no one act maliciously, and yet the groundwater level is still dropping? Because tap water in Munich isn’t priced according to how scarce it is at any given time. A cubic meter costs the same in July as it does in January, whether or not it rains. There’s no feedback loop between the individual decision made at the faucet and the condition of the aquifer—no price, no indicator, no signal. That is precisely what makes each individual decision rational and the sum of all individual decisions destructive.

This is exacerbated by the climate. The winter and spring of 2026 were exceptionally dry in Bavaria—at about three-quarters of Bavaria’s monitoring stations, groundwater levels are low to very low. When thunderstorms are now forecast, it creates the impression that the problem will resolve itself. It does not: Heavy rain runs off the surface before seeping into deeper groundwater layers. The intuition that “it’s raining, after all” is just as rational as watering at noon—and just as wrong, given the timescale over which groundwater actually recovers.

The Paradoxical Interaction of Lawn Sprinkling

The Lawn Sprinkler Paradox: An unpriced, shared resource is used rationally by each individual—and exploited by everyone collectively, without anyone intending the exploitation or even noticing it before it occurs.

Everyone acts rationally:

Citizen — waters the garden, washes the car, fills the pool (the lawn shouldn’t burn; the water “just comes out of the tap”)

City of Munich / SWM — bans rather than charges (principle of equal treatment, no price signal during ongoing operations, swift action required)

Climate — sets the new starting point (dry winter, dry spring, rain that doesn’t reach where it’s needed)

Result — structural, unintended: consumption above sustainable levels, rationing, fines, an end date that has nothing to do with the groundwater level

Everyone is to blame. No one is at fault.

What Can Be Inferred from This

What does this mean in practice—not as a solution, but as a guide? First: A general order is not a failure on the part of citizens, but an acknowledgment that there is no price signal linking individual decisions to the overall situation. Those who recognize this stop suspecting their neighbors and begin to look at the structure.

Second: The appeal—“we ask everyone”—and the ban are two different things mentioned in the same press release. The ban is effective because it has consequences. The appeal is not effective because it has none. Both sound like the same sentence. It’s worth keeping them separate.

Third: The date August 1 is not a forecast; it is an administrative artifact. What cannot be said structurally, because it is politically unspeakable: That a resource that is becoming permanently scarcer does not need a temporary order, but rather a permanent price signal. That is the sentence that will never appear in any press release.

The order expires on August 1, 2026—unless it is extended. The water table is unaware of the date.
