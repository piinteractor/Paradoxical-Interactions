Why improvement reads as an attack an established system has to repel — and why Paradoxical Interactions is no exception.

"These impressions of Dr. Semmelweis's findings lead me to the provisional judgment that his views are not clear enough, and his findings not exact enough, to qualify as scientifically established."

— Carl Edvard Marius Levy, Head of the Copenhagen Maternity Institute, 1848

26. July 2026

A system that has succeeded so far carries its own unimprovability like a flag — earned, it believes, by its own merit. Suggestions for improvement arriving from outside register as an annoyance: reinterpret them, dilute them, file them away. An innovation that lands precisely on the mark is harder to shrug off. It has to be repelled. Not because it's wrong. Because it's too precise — and precision insults the intelligence the system has already claimed.

Vienna, 1847

Two maternity wards, one hospital. In the first, women die of childbed fever at alarming rates. In the second, almost never. The difference: the first is staffed by physicians who walk straight from the dissection room to the delivery bed. The second is run by midwives — who have no contact with corpses at all. The physician Ignaz Semmelweis has a hunch: residue from the autopsy table, carried on the doctors' hands. He introduces chlorinated-lime washing. Mortality drops to a fraction of what it was.

This is not a vague hygiene tip. This is a precise causal mechanism with an unambiguous culprit: the doctor himself.

The Mechanism

A system doesn't process anything directly. Everything arriving from outside gets translated into its own code, by its own distinctions — otherwise it can neither understand nor be understood. A vague explanation is easy to reinterpret: many readings fit, none commits to anything. A precise explanation doesn't dilute so easily. It names the place, the cause, the responsible party. Exactly where it lands, the receiving system has nowhere left to dodge to.

As long as the flaw stays unnamed, it stays quietly in the background — no proof that the system doesn't work. Only the hit on the mark, the naming, turns it into an uncomfortable fact with an address, a date, a name, an origin. Vague criticism misses. Precise criticism can cost you your reputation.

Levy's verdict on Semmelweis — "not exact enough" — is not a misjudgment. It is the only available defense: dismissal. You cannot refute an exact diagnosis without refuting it exactly. So you declare it imprecise instead. The label ships the fault back to where it came from.

The Precision Ban

The Precision Ban: A system accepts an external explanation in direct proportion to its vagueness — and rejects it in direct proportion to its precision. Truth doesn't decide acceptance. The recipient's status does.

Everyone acts rationally:

Levy — rejects the study (protects his own professional authority)

Klein, Semmelweis's superior — clings to miasma theory (protects institutional interpretive authority)

Semmelweis — insists on the exact formulation (the only one that saves lives)

Outcome — total rejection despite a tenfold drop in mortality, structural, unintended

All are guilty. None are at fault.

Semmelweis died in 1865 under murky circumstances in an asylum. Recognition came posthumously, after the development of germ theory — once his precise observations belonged not to him anymore, but to science itself.

The Demigod in White

The rejection wasn't a nineteenth-century slip. It became procedure. Current meta-analyses of hand hygiene in hospitals put physician compliance at around 45 percent — lower than nurses, at around 52 percent. The gap isn't about knowledge. Research on "physician defiance" describes, explicitly, a gap between what doctors know about hand hygiene and what they do — one that closes mainly under observation, rarely out of conviction.

Semmelweis delivered the knowledge in 1847. Almost 180 years later, the position is still standing. Whoever needs an order instead of a conviction never accepted the diagnosis — only the supervision. The demigod in white was never refuted. He submitted to oversight instead, without changing.

Navigation, Not Solution

There's no formulation that gets around the Precision Ban. Explaining more sharply doesn't help — it only hardens the resistance, because it lands even more precisely on the defender's shortcomings. Explaining more gently doesn't help either — it makes the explanation noncommittal enough to be filed under "very interesting," set aside, and forgotten. Both paths end at the same point: irrelevance. Your own conviction is not a variable you can transfer to someone else. The more convinced you are, the less it transfers.

What's left is scattering without expectation — planting seeds without controlling where they take root: the Dandelion Strategy. Not because it's more comfortable. Because it's the only position that offers the mechanism no further resistance. It succeeds by not expecting success — which is exactly why it tends to work.

The Actual Insight

The same holds for PI itself.

Paradoxical Interactions describes structures with names, actors, exact mechanisms — the very precision this text has just described. So the theory's own logic forces the conclusion: PI is resisted most decisively by exactly the systems whose mark it hits most precisely. Not because it's imprecise. Because it's too precise.

That's not a reservation about PI. It's the only confirmation a theory can get for its own unacceptability: it predicts its own rejection — and gets rejected.

The demigods in white still wear their unholy title. The question that remains open: what, exactly, makes up the non-divine half?
