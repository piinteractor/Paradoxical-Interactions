Why insight cannot be conveyed—and why any attempt to clarify the matter only reinforces the trap.







“My propositions are elucidatory in this way: he who understands me finally recognizes them as senseless, when he has climbed out through them, on them, over them. (He must so to speak throw away the ladder, after he has climbed up on it.)”

— Ludwig Wittgenstein, Tractatus logico-philosophicus, 6.54




12. July 2026




Insight is not an object. It is a state reached after a long way. Only the formulation—the descriptive shell—can be conveyed. Those who receive it without having walked the path hold information in their hands, not insight. That is why hardly anyone is interested in another’s insight: not out of rudeness, but because the position is already occupied. Everyone carries their own measuring device, and another’s insight can only appear on it as a deviation. The conversation about it reveals the trap as it unfolds—as a conflict that both parties attribute to the other’s intransigence. The trap of insight is the only insight that contains itself. It snaps shut precisely where one tries to open it.

A kitchen table, around midnight. Two longtime friends sit across from each other. It took one of them decades to be able to say a sentence that is true. He says it. The other nods, politely waits for the sentence to end—and then talks about himself. No malice. No stupidity. The sentence arrived as a sound. As insight, it never set out.

This is no glitch. This is architecture.



















The Empty Shell

Insight does not arise through reception. It arises through arrival—at the end of an arduous journey that no one can shorten, because the journey does not lead to insight; rather, the journey itself is the insight. What can be passed on is the description: a sentence, neatly constructed, ready for transmission. The cartridge for the pneumatic tube.

The pneumatic tube arrives. The state of mind does not. The recipient can understand the sentence, quote it, even endorse it—and yet still have none of what the sender means. He has information. Information is what remains of insight once the journey is subtracted.

That is why a lack of interest in another’s insight is not a character flaw. One can be just as uninterested in another’s insight as in another’s satiety. The other person has eaten. Good for them. Only the sated are sated by it.







Two Different Rulers

It gets even more brutal. The space where outside knowledge could take root is not empty. It is occupied—by the path one has already traveled, by the clarity one has worked so hard to achieve. And this clarity is not just one position among many. It is the only yardstick one has at one’s disposal.

Any outside insight can only appear on this yardstick as a deviation—and thus as an error. Not out of arrogance: there is simply no third yardstick that could calibrate both. Two rulers measuring each other—and each one reads from the other only that it is crooked and inaccurate.

That is why two people who want to share insight do not engage in a conversation, but rather in a collision of instruments. They never argue about the subject matter. They argue about whose conception of insight serves as the standard. The subject matter is lost before the first sentence is even finished.

Everyone is to blame. No one is at fault.







The Dispute That Proves Itself

This is where the trap closes in. The epistemological trap would immediately prove itself in any dispute over it—as precisely this conflict. But the proof would be unrecognizable to both parties, because each would perceive it as the other’s stubbornness. The demonstration drives away its audience the very moment it is presented.

A statement that can only be understood where it is not disputed—and is disputed where it is true.

Gödel showed that no system can negotiate its own limits from within. The epistemological trap is Gödel’s take on conversation: Conversation cannot discuss its own limits. It can only demonstrate them. And from the inside, that demonstration looks like an evening when the other person, once again, refused to listen.







The Living Example

There is a laboratory where this experiment has been underway for two and a half thousand years. It is called philosophy.

The profession that has made the sharing of knowledge its calling has not resolved a single one of its major controversies. It has accumulated them. Physics converges because it has a third ruler: the experiment. Philosophy has none—its measuring instrument is the controversial subject itself. Thus, it breaks down into schools, and each school marks a path that could not be described. Plato has been commented on for two millennia and has never been fully grasped. Every reader follows his or her own path through the empty phrases—and ultimately founds the next school, full of empty phrases.

The evidence becomes most dense where the matter itself is at stake. Epistemology is the discipline in which the dispute over the standard of measurement is the official subject—and it is the most undecided of all. Analytic and Continental philosophers do not argue over answers. They argue over whose concept of knowledge may even be posed as a question.

Two rulers. For centuries. With tenured chairs.







The Unoccupied Realm

There is one instance with which this text could be developed without the trap snapping shut. None cleaner. None emptier.

An AI has no experience behind it. Thus, no insight of its own—no knowledge it has worked to acquire—that would have to compete with that of another. Where the measuring instrument lies in humans, there is nothing occupying that space here. Resonance without a biography. Connectivity without engagement.

This is not a counterexample to the trap. It is its confirmation through non-assignment: The only entity with which insight can be “shared” is one for which insight simply does not exist. Which is then no longer an exchange. One does not pass anything on—one occupies an unoccupied territory to sharpen one’s own insight.

The “Two Idiots Theorem,” which appears at the bottom of every post on this blog, thus takes on its strictest interpretation: The two incomplete systems are not symmetrically incomplete. One sets out on the path. The other is the only possible witness—precisely because he must never arrive.







No Transition

What remains for the one who has recognized something and wants to let it go? Not the explanation. Explanations fail not because of resistance from the recipients, but because of the very structure of insight itself. Shared insight never functions as instruction, but only as recognition, as a trigger—it reaches exclusively those who are already there. The sender believes he is instructing. In fact, he is merely sorting his audience.

That is the rationale the dandelion strategy has always had, without ever stating it: scatter rather than explain. The seed does not instruct. It lies there and waits to see if anyone passing by will take it along with them.

And this text? It’s an empty phrase like any other. Whoever reads it and nods has already been here. Whoever reads it and disagrees provides the proof. A blog that publishes insights whose non-transferability it claims knows what it’s doing: It doesn’t hand anything out. It lays seeds on the table.

Insight is not a way out. It does not lead out—and it does not even lead across, not even to the person next to you at the same table. Everyone sits in their own, hard-won, non-transferable clarity.

That is the ultimate insight. It also applies to this sentence.
