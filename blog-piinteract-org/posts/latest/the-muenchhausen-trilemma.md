Why the auditor independent enough to catch your blind spot would need a standard that gives it a blind spot of its own.

"...by my own plait of hair, together with the horse, which I locked firmly between my knees."

— Rudolf Erich Raspe, The Surprising Adventures of Baron Munchausen (1785)

This article continues the hypothesis of “The Missing Organ.” If a system cannot reliably detect its own blind spot, perhaps an outside observer can. The Muenchhausen trilemma asks what would justify that observer — and closes every exit.

12. August 2026

The Setup

The Baron falls into a swamp. Horse and rider are sinking. There is no branch, no rope, no one on the bank.

So he grabs his own pigtail and pulls himself — horse between his knees — out of the mud.

The trick is impossible for a simple reason. The force remains inside the same system. Pull and counter-pull act on the same body. Pulling harder changes nothing.

What is missing is not strength.

What is missing is a point of leverage outside the system.

The Missing Organ asked whether a system could detect a blind spot produced by its own architecture. The obvious answer seemed to be: bring in an outside observer.

But what qualifies that observer to judge?

This is where the swamp gets deeper.

Three Ways Out, All of Them Closed

In 1968, German philosopher Hans Albert gave the problem its modern name: the Muenchhausen trilemma.

Any attempt at ultimate justification ends in one of three places.

A claim is justified by another claim. That claim requires another justification, and so on indefinitely: infinite regress.

Or the chain eventually returns to something it already used: circular reasoning.

Or it stops somewhere and declares a premise self-evident, fundamental, or beyond further justification: dogmatism.

Regress. Circle. Assumption.

There is no final foundation that does not eventually encounter one of them.

The name is precise. Like the Baron, justification tries to pull itself out of the swamp by its own hair.

Agrippa Got There First

The structure is much older than Albert.

Roughly two thousand years earlier, the skeptical modes attributed to Agrippa and preserved by Sextus Empiricus already included regress, circularity, and unsupported assumption among the reasons for suspending judgment.

The ancient formulation was broader, but the family resemblance is unmistakable.

That matters because the problem is not a peculiarity of modern science, AI, or institutional auditing.

It is a problem of justification itself.

Who certifies the certifier?

The Auditor Who Cannot Remain Innocent

Now return to the outside observer.

Suppose we construct the cleanest auditor imaginable: no involvement in the system, no interests, no prior commitment to its conclusions.

It still cannot judge without a criterion.

What counts as correct?

What counts as biased?

What counts as a blind spot?

The auditor needs a standard.

And immediately the trilemma returns.

If the standard comes from prior knowledge or training, the auditor now carries its own formation and therefore its own possible blind spots.

If the standard is simply declared valid, justification stops in an assumption.

If another observer validates the standard, that observer requires another standard.

The outside observer has not solved the problem.

It has moved the problem.

An observer with no prior structure might be free of one source of bias.

But without prior structure, it also has nothing with which to judge.

The same thing that makes judgment possible makes neutral judgment impossible.

The Right-Instance PI

Everyone acts rationally:

The audited system reports what it can observe from within its own structure.

The outside auditor requires criteria; without them, there is no audit.

Those criteria derive from prior assumptions, other criteria, or further justification.

The institution accepts the auditor because some stopping point is operationally necessary.

Result: independence can be increased, but never finally certified from nowhere.

All are guilty. None are at fault.

Navigate, Don't Solve

Albert did not offer a fourth door.

Critical rationalism makes a different move: abandon the demand for final justification. Treat claims as provisional, criticizable, corrigible.

That changes the role of the outside observer.

We no longer need one perfect auditor standing outside the swamp.

We need different imperfect observers whose blind spots do not completely coincide.

One may catch what another cannot. A third may expose assumptions shared by the first two. None occupies an absolute outside position. Each introduces another perspective into the relation.

This is not final verification.

It is reciprocal correction.

And that returns us to the point where The Missing Organ ended.

Mariotte needed another configuration to reveal the blind spot. The neurological patient needs another observer. A formal argument can be examined from another formal position. An institution can be audited by another institution.

But the second observer has blind spots too.

So does the third.

The Baron cannot pull himself out by pulling harder.

But perhaps the objective was never to find someone standing completely outside the swamp.

Perhaps it is enough that we are not all sinking in exactly the same place.
